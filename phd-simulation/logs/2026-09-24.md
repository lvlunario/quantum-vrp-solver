## 08:30–09:30 — A formulation is not yet a circuit
The fictional morning starts with yesterday's stricter requirement: all optimal decisions should satisfy the capacity constraint. The piecewise overload penalty remains easy to evaluate in ordinary Python, but that convenience hides how it would be represented for a quantum optimization circuit. Today's goal is therefore deliberately limited. Produce an explicit quadratic binary expression and prove that it represents the same decision problem after auxiliary variables are handled correctly.

You resist adding forecasts, batteries or multiple time periods. Each would be plausible in a real microgrid, but each would multiply the ways a model could fail before the small example is understood. Your old integration habit is useful here: stabilize one interface before connecting the next subsystem. The interface today is between the original service decisions and their encoded energy function.

## 09:30–11:30 — Coursework through a concrete derivation
Unused capacity becomes a slack quantity. If demand is two, slack must be three; if demand is five, slack must be zero. Three binary variables with weights one, two and four represent slack from zero through seven. Capacity is enforced through the squared residual of demand plus slack minus five. Because a binary variable squared equals itself, expanding this square creates only constant, linear and pairwise terms.

The [derivation note](../docs/qubo-equivalence.md) records the complete coefficient convention. Cross terms are counted once, using an upper-triangular representation. This avoids a familiar implementation trap: building a symmetric matrix and accidentally counting pairwise contributions twice. At penalty two the constant is 64, and the six linear coefficients are negative 35, 47, 54, 18, 32 and 48 respectively. The raw result records every pairwise coefficient without relying on rounded prose.

## 11:30–12:00 — Lunch and a resource question
In the story, a short break raises an uncomfortable tradeoff. The original model had three decision bits; the quadratic formulation now has six total bits. A cleaner algebraic structure can cost additional variables. That is not evidence against quantum optimization, but it prevents an honest comparison from treating the two formulations as identical computational tasks. No circuit resource estimate is claimed yet.

## 12:00–14:00 — Actual exhaustive verification
The catch-up audit evaluates all 64 six-bit assignments. For each, it compares the expanded polynomial with the original squared-residual expression. It then minimizes over slack for each of the eight service decisions and compares the resulting energy with the original overload penalty. Both checks pass. At penalty two, the unique ground state serves the first two loads with zero slack and total energy six.

The proof explains why this works rather than merely announcing agreement. Every feasible demand has an exactly representable nonnegative slack. For overload, adding nonnegative slack can only increase the already positive residual, so zero slack is best. Unused representations six and seven cannot improve the minimum. These claims depend on the present integer inputs and capacity; they are not a substitute for specifying ranges on future instances.

## 14:00–15:00 — Deliberate failures
Two faults are intentionally introduced into the audit. Restricting slack to two bits excludes the required slack five for the all-off decision. Its reduced energy becomes 22 instead of 14, even though the optimal service choice remains unchanged. This shows why checking only the final optimum is weaker than checking formulation equivalence. Omitting all pairwise terms produces disagreements on 57 of the 64 assignments.

These are executed fault-injection examples, not invented stories about accidental bugs. Neither count is a general detection-rate estimate. The selected defects are simple and known in advance; a broader study would need a justified fault catalog and independent evaluation instances.
